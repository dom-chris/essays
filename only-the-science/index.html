Curiosity, intellectual audacity, uncertainty, and the discipline of changing one’s mind.

An Impressionist Portrait

To write a portrait is to select: to look back across a life and say, among all that happened, this seems to matter; to notice a recurrence and wonder whether it was circumstance — or character.

What follows is therefore not biography in the exhaustive sense, but neither is it allegory. The path was real. The choices were made. The words were spoken.

What follows is speculation.

Not speculation as fantasy, but as commitment under uncertainty: the uneasy, happy point at which the picture remains incomplete, yet the pattern has become too strong to ignore.

One does not know. But one has seen enough to take a position.

Science Through Letters

At the beginning of the twentieth century, physics was coming apart.

Planck had introduced the quantum. Einstein had made light behave like particles. Lorentz, Poincaré, Marie Curie and the other giants of European physics were trying to understand what exactly they had unleashed.

In 1911, many of them gathered in Brussels for the first Solvay Conference. Among those close to the proceedings was a young French experimental physicist named Maurice.

He belonged there. His younger brother did not.

The younger man had studied literature and history. Physics was not yet his profession, much less his destiny.

But Maurice had been closely involved in recording the extraordinary discussions at Solvay and preparing their proceedings, and his younger brother was drawn into that work — reading the arguments, following the debates, encountering through them some of the greatest scientific minds of the age.

The historian crossed the border. He had entered physics as a man of letters: through words, through arguments, through a document. He read, he listened, and something caught.

Curiosity is common enough, but curiosity usually remains recreational. We read about another subject, admire it, perhaps speak about it.

He did something else.

He entered it.

His first degree was in history. Three years later he had a degree in science, for he had true ambition, the ambition to understand.

The Darkening

Then Europe went dark.

War.

His studies stopped. He entered the army and spent much of the First World War working in wireless communications from the Eiffel Tower.

The future theoretical physicist found himself dealing with apparatus, transmission, reception and radio.

From the Eiffel Tower, he intercepted German communications.

Already, in a way, it was about waves.

The war interrupted his curriculum but not his education.

The theoretician learned something of the practical world. The younger brother of an experimental physicist handled technology rather than merely equations, and even an interruption became material.

When peace returned, so did he.

The Latecomer

And then the latecomer moved very quickly.

He began thinking about one of the strangest facts emerging from the new physics. Light, long understood as a wave, could behave like particles.

What if the symmetry ran both ways? What if matter, which appeared to us as particles, could also behave like waves?

It was an audacious thought, and he developed it in his doctoral thesis.

Soon electron diffraction would confirm the wave behaviour of matter, while Schrödinger would build upon the idea in developing wave mechanics.

And in 1929, only five years after the doctorate, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for the discovery of the wave nature of electrons.

The young historian, the soldier at the Eiffel Tower, the latecomer who had entered physics through the intellectual world of his elder brother, was Louis de Broglie.

That would already be enough for a remarkable life.

It is not, however, the principal reason I admire him.

The Theory He Let Go

Two years before the Nobel Prize, de Broglie had returned to Solvay.

In 1911 he had encountered the great problems of physics from the margins; in 1927 he was looked upon as one of the men expected to solve them.

Quantum mechanics had become astonishingly successful, but with that success came an unsettling interpretation. At the deepest level, reality seemed not merely difficult to predict, but fundamentally probabilistic.

“God does not play dice” became the famous shorthand for Einstein’s resistance to the idea that probability was the final layer of reality.

De Broglie resisted.

He proposed instead that the particle remained real, that it had a position and followed a trajectory, while the quantum wave guided it. What appeared to us as randomness might therefore conceal an underlying order.

The idea was challenged, and challenged hard. De Broglie defended it, but eventually concluded that he could not answer the objections well enough.

So he let it go.

There is a familiar image of intellectual courage: the lone genius stands against the crowd, everybody tells him he is wrong, he refuses to bend, and eventually history vindicates him.

Sometimes courage looks like that.

But sometimes courage looks almost exactly opposite.

De Broglie had every possible reason to become attached to his theory. He had already transformed physics once, his name carried authority, and he was about to receive its greatest honour. He could have spent decades defending his intellectual territory.

Instead, when he believed the argument had moved against him, he moved with it.

He abandoned his interpretation and, for roughly a quarter of a century, taught the prevailing one.

Not his own, but the theory that had defeated his.

He had been ambitious enough to propose a new account of reality, but not vain enough to require reality to agree with him.

Outside of the Box

Perhaps that quality belonged to something wider in the intellectual culture which had produced him.

The physicists of that extraordinary generation often seemed remarkably uninterested in remaining inside the boxes later assigned to them.

And not Schrödinger's box but the disciplinary ones.

Wolfgang Pauli could spend years exchanging ideas with Carl Gustav Jung, trying to understand whether physics and psychology might illuminate one another. Niels Bohr would place the yin-yang on his coat of arms beneath the motto Contraria sunt complementa — opposites are complementary.

Let us be clear: Quantum mechanics was not produced by mysticism; Bohr did not derive complementarity from Taoism, nor did Pauli discover physics through Jung.

But perhaps there is still something worth noticing. These were minds remarkably unafraid of trespass. Physics, philosophy, psychology, mathematics, consciousness and metaphysics — the borders existed, but they were not walls.

It is difficult not to wonder whether this freedom of movement had something to do with the extraordinary audacity of their science.

In only a few decades, humanity radically altered its understanding of matter, light, space, time and causality. There have been immense advances since, but it is difficult to think of another period in modern science in which so much of the conceptual furniture of reality was overturned so quickly.

Another Strange Mind

Then, some twenty-five years after de Broglie abandoned his theory, another unusual physicist appeared.

David Bohm.

Bohm too was difficult to keep inside one box. He had already done important work in plasma physics, studying systems in which enormous numbers of charged particles could display collective behaviour.

But his intellectual interests ranged much further. Physics led into philosophy, philosophy into consciousness, and consciousness into psychology.

For years he held conversations with the Indian thinker Jiddu Krishnamurti about thought, the observer, conditioning, perception and whether the mind could ever see without distorting what it saw.

He was, in other words, another one of those minds apparently unaware that intellectual subjects were supposed to remain in separate rooms.

And in 1952, Bohm returned to the road de Broglie had abandoned.

He showed that a deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics had not been killed after all. Particles could have definite positions, follow trajectories and be guided by a quantum wave.

The old theory lived.

One can imagine how a lesser man might have reacted: vindication, twenty-five years of suppressed resentment suddenly released, an enormous I told you so.

But de Broglie did something more characteristic: he looked again.

He was not even immediately persuaded. He reconsidered the arguments and then, gradually, returned to the causal interpretation he had abandoned decades earlier.

Constancy

He changed his mind twice.

The historian became a physicist. The theoretician became a soldier dealing with radio equipment. The latecomer became a Nobel laureate. The radical became a teacher of orthodoxy. And the man who abandoned his theory returned to it when the argument changed again.

His opinions moved. His character did not.

De Broglie could believe something strongly without needing the belief to become part of himself. He could lose an argument without experiencing the loss as an injury, teach an opposing theory without imagining that doing so diminished the greatness of his own work, and return to an old idea without pretending that he had never doubted it.

Perhaps this is also where the story returns to the speculation with which we began.

We rarely possess the whole picture, yet neither are we condemned to ignorance. Somewhere between certainty and randomness lies judgment: enough pattern to justify conviction, enough uncertainty to keep conviction humble.

That is the uneasy happiness of speculation.

One commits. But one remains movable.

And that, perhaps, was de Broglie's deeper consistency: not fidelity to a particular conclusion, but fidelity to the process by which conclusions deserve to be held.

Only the Science

We often imagine intellectual greatness as enormous confidence.

De Broglie suggests another possibility: to think greatly may require enormous curiosity, extraordinary ambition and the courage to cross borders others regard as fixed.

He once gave a wonderfully severe instruction to a man who wanted to write about him.

No biographical trivia.

Seule, la Science.

Only the science.

It is a remarkable request, and perhaps an impossible one.

Because in trying to remove the man from the story, he revealed something about the man.

The argument mattered more than the author, the discovery more than the discoverer.

The truth more than the ownership of it.
