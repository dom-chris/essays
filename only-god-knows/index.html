Only God Knows

A question about divine knowledge becomes a question about revelation—and the identity of Jesus.

Scroll to read ↓

Only the Father?

Among the challenges to the orthodox Christian understanding of Jesus, one of the most familiar begins with a sentence in the Gospel of Mark. Speaking about the end of the age, Jesus says of the day and the hour:

“No one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.”

From these words, two related objections are commonly raised, and they should be distinguished from the outset.

The first turns on “nor the Son.” If Jesus does not know the hour, He is not omniscient; and if He is not omniscient, He cannot be God. This objection directly challenges the divinity of Christ.

The second turns on “but only the Father.” The Father knows what the Son does not. If Father and Son are equally divine, how can their knowledge differ? This raises a broader challenge to the Christian doctrine of the Trinity.

We shall take them in turn, beginning with the first and the question of divine knowledge.

Christians have offered various answers. One invokes a Jewish wedding custom in which the bridegroom knows the wedding approaches, but only the father determines or announces its precise moment. It is an attractive explanation, although its historical foundations are disputed. Another is linguistic: the Greek verb is oida, ordinarily “to know,” but some argue that it here means something closer to “make known” or “declare.” That reading, too, is contested.

Perhaps one of these explanations is correct. But let us assume, for the moment, that neither is. Let us allow “does not know” to retain its ordinary force and take the first objection at full strength.

If its reasoning is sound, it cannot apply to Jesus alone. Language of non-knowledge that excludes Jesus from divinity in Mark must have the same consequence when comparable language appears elsewhere in Scripture.

So let us leave Mark’s Gospel for a moment.

Now I Know

Genesis, the opening book of the Torah, tells one of its most famous stories.

God tests Abraham by commanding him to sacrifice Isaac, the son he loves. Abraham takes him up the mountain, binds him, and raises the knife. At the final moment he is stopped, and from heaven come the words:

“Now I know that you fear God, seeing you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.”

Now I know.

What was known before?

Earlier in Genesis, the language goes further still. The sin of Sodom and Gomorrah has already been committed. The LORD says:

“The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is so great and their sin so grievous that I will go down and see if what they have done is as bad as the outcry that has reached me. If not, I will know.”

Read with the same literalness applied to Mark, the apparent limitation concerns not knowledge alone but presence. God is described as though He were somewhere rather than everywhere. An outcry reaches Him; He must go down, see for Himself, and then He will know.

These are no marginal stories. At the centre of both stands Abraham, a figure so foundational to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam that the three are commonly called the Abrahamic religions. Nor is this language confined to the Torah. It appears in the Qurʾān.

The Qurʾān could hardly insist more strongly upon the completeness of Allah’s knowledge. He knows the seen and the unseen, the land and the sea. In one particularly vivid image:

“Not a leaf falls but that He knows it.”

Yet the Qurʾān can also speak very differently.

In the early Muslim community, the direction faced in prayer—called the qibla in Arabic—was changed toward the Sacred Mosque in Mecca. Islamic tradition identifies the earlier direction as Jerusalem. Describing the purpose of that earlier direction, the Qurʾān says that it was appointed:

“that We might know who follows the Messenger from him who turns back on his heels.”

Elsewhere the language is even more striking:

“We shall surely test you until We know those of you who strive and are steadfast.”

The Arabic uses ordinary forms of the verb ʿalima: to know. Some translations and interpretations give the theological sense as “make evident,” “distinguish,” or “prove”—the same kind of interpretative move encountered in debates over Mark. Classical Islamic interpretation does not conclude that Allah learns through these tests. The events make manifest within time what God already knows.

The settings differ, but the same principle is being tested. The God of the Torah says, Now I know; of the Son, the Gospel says He does not know; Allah says in the Qurʾān, Until We know.

If genuine ignorance is incompatible with divinity, the question cannot stop with Jesus. The scriptures themselves force it wider.

And so we can ask: What does it mean for God to know?

What Does It Mean for God to Know?

These scriptural scenes present a striking paradox. Read with the same literalness applied to Mark, they depict a God who learns, tests, and observes. Yet across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, divine omniscience is non-negotiable. These passages do not stand in a vacuum; they exist alongside explicit scriptural affirmations of exhaustive divine knowledge, resting upon a philosophical baseline that extends far beyond the Abrahamic traditions.

Long before these theological disputes, Greek philosophy arrived at that same baseline. Analyzing the physical world, Aristotle observed that whatever changes is set in motion by something else. To avoid an infinite regress, reason demands a First Cause—an Unmoved Mover—that initiates all change without undergoing change itself. For such a Cause, there can be no shift from ignorance to awareness, no unfulfilled potentiality, and no new information to acquire.

Classical theology integrated this insight to articulate God’s relationship to the cosmos. God is not a supreme actor moving alongside us inside the universe. He is the transcendent source and sustainer of reality itself. Because He is not carried through successive moments, He does not wait for tomorrow to discover what it holds. Nor can He receive reports from an independent creation, or wait for Abraham to raise the knife before discovering his intent. A God who learns from His creatures would be acted upon by them—a caused being, rather than the First Cause.

Yet here the premise opens into mystery. We understand human knowing because we live it. Something exists outside us; we encounter, perceive, measure, or infer it. Human knowledge has a before and an after—it is receptive, successive, and temporal. But creaturely knowing cannot simply be projected onto the Creator. We can easily state the proposition: Nothing true is unknown to God. But what is it actually to know without discovery, observation, memory, surprise, succession, or temporal location?

Reason takes us to the attribute; it does not take us inside it. We may establish that God must be omniscient without discovering what it is for God to know.

This distinction is easily lost because words create an illusion of possession. We say omniscient, eternal, or omnipotent, and quietly imagine that infinite reality has been reduced to a concept fitting inside our minds. But a true statement about God is not the same thing as comprehension of God.

Mystery

Mystery is often treated as theology’s embarrassment—a retreat invoked when explanation fails or contradiction threatens.

Perhaps this gets things almost exactly backwards.

If God really is God, mystery is not an unfortunate remainder left by insufficient knowledge. It belongs necessarily to a finite creature’s knowledge of the Creator. Reason can travel from creation toward Him, inferring His necessity and excluding logical contradictions. But inference alone cannot give man direct access to the divine essence.

At some point, the direction must reverse: from inference, moving from the world toward God, to revelation, moving from God toward the world.

Yet even revelation does not abolish mystery. It conveys real truth and tells us what we could never otherwise have known, but the infinite does not thereby become finite. God does not become an object the human intellect can dismantle, reconstruct, and comprehend from within.

Revelation gives knowledge.

It does not give mastery.

One of religion’s most persistent temptations is to mistake familiarity with words for mastery over reality. Then Scripture says something strange—Now I know, The Son does not know, Until We know—and our conceptual model breaks. Perhaps revelation has not described God badly; perhaps we quietly assumed we already knew what divine knowing must look like.

There is a characteristically Islamic expression of intellectual humility: Allāhu aʿlam—“God knows best.” It is usually invoked where human knowledge is uncertain. Here, however, it acquires a far deeper resonance.

At the outset, only God knows appeared as a polemical claim against the divinity of Christ: God knows the Hour; Jesus does not; therefore Jesus cannot be God.

Now the phrase means something deeper: Only God knows as God knows.

We can affirm omniscience without standing inside it to inspect its mode. Receiving revelation is not enclosing the divine essence within the human mind. Even when God reveals Himself, the infinite remains greater than the mind receiving Him.

That is mystery.

The Early Christians

The limits of finite understanding were no abstract question for the early Church. Christian writers had to interpret Mark’s words within a faith that already confessed both Father and Son.

Writing around AD 180, Irenaeus discusses the passage without apparent embarrassment. The Son, he notes, “ascribed the knowledge of that day to the Father only.” Rather than treating this as a threat to Christ’s divinity, Irenaeus turns the verse against human presumption: if the Son leaves certain knowledge with the Father, how much less should human beings claim to penetrate the hidden things of God?

A generation later, Tertullian gives the verse a different use. Writing against Praxeas, who collapsed Father and Son into a single person, he cites the Father’s knowledge and the Son’s ignorance as evidence of their distinction.

Later controversy required greater precision. Athanasius related the saying to Christ’s humanity; Hilary understood the ignorance as concealment; Augustine interpreted “not knowing” as “not making known.” These were not inventions of a new object of faith, but attempts to state more exactly how the received confession of Christ accommodated the words in Mark.

A word need not exist before the reality it names has been encountered. The first Christians received and confessed Father, Son, and Spirit before the Church possessed the complete technical vocabulary of the later councils. Controversy did not create that faith; it required the Church to distinguish and defend more exactly what it had already received.

One of the words by which the Church learned to guard that faith was:

Trinity.

Trinity

The Trinity is not an emergency explanation invented to rescue Mark 13:32. It is the doctrinal grammar by which Christianity names and guards the larger scriptural reality in which Mark 13:32 already stands.

Christians do not read this verse in isolation. Only a few lines earlier, the same chapter identifies the Son who does not know the Hour as the Son of Man—the one who comes “in clouds with great power and glory,” sends his angels to gather his elect, and speaks words that outlast heaven and earth. The saying stands firmly within that transcendent identity.

This brings us directly to the second objection: the difference in knowledge between Father and Son.

The Father knows.

The Son does not.

Understanding how this can be said of the Son while the Father knows requires both Trinity and Incarnation. Christianity claims not only that Father and Son are distinct, but that the eternal Son became genuinely human. Jesus hungers, sleeps, suffers, and dies. Yet Christian theology does not conclude that God as God requires food, experiences fatigue, or can be destroyed. Mark 13:32 must therefore be read within the Christian claim of Incarnation as well as Trinity.

This does not make the mystery vanish; it defines which mystery we are attempting to understand. The interpretations already encountered—whether relating the saying to Christ’s humanity, understanding the ignorance as concealment, or interpreting “not knowing” as “not making known”—are all efforts to read the verse within this broader framework. A single clause about knowledge cannot be isolated from Christ’s full identity and made to decide His divinity in a vacuum.

The verse has force. It poses a genuine theological question, but it has no independent power to overturn the larger scriptural account in which it stands.

Trinity and Incarnation locate the mystery within the life of the incarnate Son and between the divine Persons. They do not allow us to inspect divine knowing from within. Here Allāhu aʿlam applies with full force: only God knows God from within.

If creatures cannot climb inside the divine essence, whatever lies beyond what creation can teach must come from the other direction.

Who Is the Son?

The Christian confession holds that Jesus is truly God and truly man: the eternal Son who assumed human existence without ceasing to be divine.

To call this union a mystery is no Christological dodge. As we have seen with divine knowledge itself, mystery is the baseline condition of all creaturely thought about the Creator. If we cannot comprehend how God knows without sequence or change, we should hardly expect the union of divine and human natures to be any less transcendent. The mystery of the Incarnation concerns the mode of that union, not the clarity of who Christ is confessed to be.

Only God can reveal God—and that revelation must be received in its fullness. Mark 13:32 cannot be severed from its scriptural setting. The verse belongs to Mark 13, the chapter to the Gospel, and the Gospel to the whole of Christian Scripture.

Within that whole, Mark 13:32 witnesses to Christ’s humanity, while its immediate context and the wider Scriptures disclose His divine authority. Far from erasing the verse, Trinity and Incarnation locate it within that complete scriptural witness.

The debate has therefore shifted. The question is no longer whether a single verse, isolated from its context, disproves the divinity of Christ. The serious challenge to Christianity lies in confronting the full identity disclosed across the Scriptures and asking whether it is true—whether this is indeed what God has revealed concerning Jesus.

Mark 13:32 does not close the case on Christ’s divinity. It brings us to the threshold of revelation itself.
