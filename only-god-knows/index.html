Only God Knows

A question about divine knowledge becomes a question about revelation—and the identity of Jesus.

Scroll to read ↓

Among the objections raised against the divinity of Christ, one of the most familiar begins with a sentence in the Gospel of Mark.

Jesus is speaking about the end of the age. Of the day and the hour, he says:

“No one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.”

The argument follows easily. If Jesus is God, he must be omniscient. If there is something he does not know, he is not omniscient. And if he is not omniscient, he cannot be God.

It is a powerful argument.

Perhaps too powerful.

Christians have offered various answers. One invokes a Jewish wedding custom in which the bridegroom knows that the wedding approaches, but only the father determines or announces its precise moment. It is an attractive explanation, although its historical foundations are disputed.

Another is linguistic. The Greek verb is oida, ordinarily “to know,” but it has been argued that the expression may here mean something closer to “make known” or “declare.” That reading too has been contested.

Perhaps one of these explanations is correct.

But let us assume, for the moment, that neither is. Let us take the objection at full strength.

What, exactly, is the principle behind it?

It is not specifically a principle about Jesus. It is a principle about God:

Genuine ignorance is incompatible with divinity.

That is the rule doing the work. If the Son does not know something, while God must know all things, then the Son cannot be God.

We need not yet decide whether the premise is correct. We need only apply it consistently. If a particular way of speaking about knowledge is incompatible with divinity in the Gospel of Mark, the same rule must apply when comparable language appears elsewhere in Scripture.

So let us leave the Gospel for a moment.

Now I Know

Genesis, the opening book of the Torah, the scripture of Israel, tells one of its most famous stories.

God tests Abraham by commanding him to sacrifice Isaac, the son he loves. Abraham takes him up the mountain, binds him and raises the knife. At the final moment he is stopped, and from heaven come the words:

“Now I know that you fear God, seeing you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.”

Now I know.

What was known before?

Earlier in Genesis, the language goes further still. The sin of Sodom and Gomorrah has already been committed. The LORD says:

“The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is so great and their sin so grievous that I will go down and see if what they have done is as bad as the outcry that has reached me. If not, I will know.”

Read with the same literalness applied to Mark, the limitation is not knowledge alone but presence. God is described as though He were somewhere rather than everywhere. An outcry reaches Him; He must go down, see for Himself, and then He will know.

These are no marginal stories. At the centre of both stands Abraham, a figure so foundational to Judaism, Christianity and Islam that the three are commonly called the Abrahamic religions.

And the language we have encountered is not confined to the Torah.

It appears in the Qurʾān.

The Qurʾān could hardly insist more strongly upon the completeness of Allah’s knowledge. He knows the seen and the unseen, the land and the sea. In one particularly vivid image:

“Not a leaf falls but that He knows it.”

Yet the Qurʾān can also speak very differently.

In the early history of the Muslim community, the direction of prayer — the qibla — which until then had been toward Jerusalem, was changed toward the Sacred Mosque in Mecca. Describing the purpose of the earlier direction, Allah says in the Qurʾān that it was appointed:

“that We might know who follows the Messenger from him who turns back on his heels.”

That We might know.

Elsewhere the language is even more striking:

“We shall surely test you until We know those of you who strive and are steadfast.”

Until We know.

The Arabic uses the ordinary language of knowing, forms of the verb ʿalima: to know. Some translations and interpretations give the theological sense as “make evident,” “distinguish” or “prove”—the same kind of interpretative move encountered in debates over Mark.

Classical Islamic interpretation does not conclude that Allah learns through the test. The event makes manifest within time what God already knows.

The comparison does not require the passages to be identical. In Mark, the apparent limitation is relational: the Father knows what the Son does not. In the Torah and the Qurʾān, the language unfolds through time and, in Sodom, through space: God tests or descends to see, and then God “knows.”

Different question. Same principle.

The God of the Torah says, Now I know.

Of the Son, the Gospel says, does not know.

Allah says in the Qurʾān, Until We know.

If genuine ignorance is incompatible with divinity, the question cannot stop with Jesus. The scriptures themselves force it wider.

What does it mean for God to know?

What Does It Mean for God to Know?

We can now return to the premise hidden inside the original objection.

God must be omniscient.

Judaism, Christianity and Islam all affirm it. Their scriptures affirm it. But the intuition is not confined to revelation.

Long before the theological disputes that concern us here, philosophy had reasoned from the world toward a first principle. Aristotle reasoned from motion toward an unmoved mover. Later classical thought would pursue the implications further.

On the classical account of God shared by much of Jewish, Christian and Islamic philosophical theology, God is not one being among others but the first cause upon which everything else depends. What could exist independently of Him from which He might need to acquire knowledge?

If God is the source and sustainer of all that exists, nothing can stand outside Him as an independent reality waiting to inform Him. If God is not carried with us through successive moments of time, He cannot wait for tomorrow to arrive before discovering what tomorrow contains.

There are sophisticated philosophical arguments behind those conclusions. We do not need them here.

Let us simply accept the premise with which the objection itself began:

God knows all things.

Now the interesting question begins.

What does it mean for God to know?

We know what human knowing is because we experience it. Something exists outside us; we encounter it, perceive it, remember it, measure it or infer it. Perhaps yesterday we did not know. Today we do. Human knowledge has a before and an after. It is receptive, successive and temporal.

But that is knowledge by a creature.

On this account, none of those characteristics can simply be transferred to the knowledge of the Creator.

God cannot wait for Abraham to raise the knife before discovering what Abraham will do. He cannot wait for tomorrow to arrive before knowing tomorrow. He cannot encounter creation as an independent reality which reports back to him what has happened within it.

And here the apparently simple premise begins to open into mystery.

We may know that God knows all things.

We have not thereby discovered what it is for God to know all things.

Reason has therefore produced a remarkable result. It may lead us to the necessity of omniscience while remaining incapable of comprehending the mode of omniscience.

We can understand the proposition:

Nothing true is unknown to God.

But what is it actually to know without discovery, observation, memory, surprise, succession or temporal location?

We do not know.

Reason has taken us to the attribute.

It has not taken us inside it.

And this is not peculiar to omniscience. Much of what human beings can know about God begins with what is not God. We encounter creation and reason from effect toward cause. These may be genuine conclusions.

But we are still learning something about the cause from its effects.

We are not examining the cause itself.

This distinction is easily lost because words create an illusion of possession. We say omniscient and imagine that we have grasped omniscience. We say eternal and imagine that we understand eternity. We say omnipotent and imagine that infinite power has somehow been reduced to a concept that fits inside our minds.

But a true statement about God is not the same thing as comprehension of God.

We may know that something must be true without knowing how that truth exists in God.

Mystery

We have now arrived somewhere quite different from where we began.

Mystery is often treated as theology’s embarrassment. When explanation fails, mystery is invoked. When contradiction threatens, mystery provides shelter.

Perhaps this has things almost exactly backwards.

If God really is God, mystery is not an unfortunate remainder left behind by insufficient knowledge. Mystery is necessarily part of what it means for a finite creature to know God.

Reason can travel from creation toward God. It can infer, establish, exclude contradictions and discover that certain things must be true.

But creation cannot give man direct access to the essence of its Creator.

At some point, the direction must reverse: from inference, moving from the world toward God, to gift, moving from God toward the world.

And even revelation does not abolish mystery. If God reveals himself, the revelation may be true. It may convey real knowledge. It may tell us things we could never have known otherwise.

But the infinite does not thereby become finite.

God does not become an object that the human intellect can dismantle, reconstruct and comprehend from within.

Revelation gives knowledge.

It does not give mastery.

This may be one of religion’s most persistent temptations. We speak about God so often that we begin to mistake familiarity with words for familiarity with being. We say eternal, omnipotent, omniscient, one, spirit. And because the words are ours, we begin to imagine that the realities they signify have become ours as well.

Then Scripture says something strange.

Now I know.

The Son does not know.

We shall test you until We know.

And suddenly the conceptual model breaks.

Perhaps the problem is not always that revelation has described God badly.

Perhaps sometimes it is that we had imagined we already knew what divine knowing must look like.

We did not.

We knew a word. We knew a proposition. We may even have known something true.

But we did not comprehend the mode of God’s being.

There is an old and characteristically Islamic expression of intellectual humility:

Allāhu aʿlam.

God knows best.

It is usually said where knowledge is uncertain or where judgment should remain modest. Here it acquires a deeper resonance.

At the beginning of this essay, only God knows appeared almost as a polemical claim: God knows the Hour; Jesus does not; therefore Jesus cannot be God.

Now the words have begun to mean something else.

Only God knows as God knows.

Only God possesses divine knowledge from within rather than reasoning toward it from without. We can know that God is omniscient. We cannot stand inside omniscience and inspect its mode of being.

Knowing that God is omniscient is not comprehending omniscience. Knowing that God is eternal is not comprehending eternity. Receiving revelation is not enclosing the divine essence within the human mind.

Creation can lead us to God.

Only God can reveal God.

And even when He does, the infinite remains greater than the mind receiving Him.

That is mystery.

The Early Christians

It is worth returning here to the Christians who first had to live with the words in Mark.

Around AD 180, Irenaeus discusses the passage without apparent scandal. The Son, he says, “ascribed the knowledge of that day to the Father only.” Rather than diminish Christ, Irenaeus uses the saying to rebuke human presumption: if the Son leaves certain knowledge with the Father, how much less should human beings claim to penetrate the hidden things of God?

A generation later, Tertullian makes the verse serve a different purpose. Writing against Praxeas, who collapsed Father and Son, he points to the knowledge of the Father and the ignorance of the Son as evidence of their distinction.

These Fathers do not yet use the precise language of the later councils. But they show that the verse was livable within a faith in which Father and Son were already distinct and the Son already belonged within the divine reality Christians confessed.

Later controversy required greater precision. Athanasius related the saying to Christ’s humanity; Hilary could understand the ignorance as concealment; Augustine could interpret “not knowing” as not making known. These were not inventions of a new object of faith, but attempts to state more exactly how the received confession of Christ accommodated the words in Mark.

A word need not exist before the reality it names has been encountered.

The first Christians received and confessed Father, Son and Spirit before the Church possessed the complete technical vocabulary of the later councils. Controversy did not create that faith. It required the Church to distinguish and defend more exactly what it had already received.

One of the words by which the Church learned to guard that faith was:

Trinity.

Trinity

The Trinity is not an explanation invented to rescue Mark 13:32.

It is the doctrinal grammar by which Christianity names and guards the larger scriptural reality concerning Father, Son and Spirit in which Mark 13:32 already stands.

Christians do not read this sentence in isolation. The same chapter, only a few lines earlier, identifies the Son who does not know the Hour as the Son of Man who will come “in clouds with great power and glory,” send his angels to gather his elect, and speak words that will outlast heaven and earth.

The limitation stands within that larger identity.

Yet Mark introduces a difficulty that Genesis and the Qurʾān do not.

The Father knows.

The Son does not.

The distinction matters, but it is not what creates the objection. Remove the final words—“but only the Father”—and the argument remains: the Son does not know; therefore He cannot be God. What changes is the Christian answer. Once we ask how the Son can truly not know while the Father knows, we have entered the doctrines of Trinity and Incarnation.

Christianity claims not only that Father and Son are distinct, but that the eternal Son became genuinely human. Jesus hungers. He sleeps. He suffers and dies.

Still, Christianity does not conclude that God as God requires food, requires sleep or can be destroyed.

Mark 13:32 therefore belongs within the larger Christian claim of Incarnation as well as Trinity.

That does not make the mystery disappear. It tells us what mystery we are actually trying to understand.

The later readings already encountered—humanity, concealment, not making known—are attempts to understand the saying within that framework. What Christians cannot do is detach the sentence from the wider identity attributed to Christ and allow one statement about knowledge to decide the whole question by itself.

The verse has force. It poses a genuine theological question, but it has no independent power to settle the divinity of Christ without reference to the larger account in which it stands.

And this helps explain why the earliest Fathers could allow the words to stand without alarm. For them, the verse did not overturn the larger scriptural reality in which Father and Son were already understood.

Trinity and Incarnation locate the mystery between Father and Son and within the life of the man who is the Son. They do not allow us to inspect divine knowing from within.

Here Allāhu aʿlam applies with full force.

Only God knows God from within.

And that changes the question.

If creatures cannot climb inside the divine essence and discover God for themselves, then whatever lies beyond what creation can teach us must come from the other direction.

Therefore only God can reveal God.

Revelation

Once the limits of reason are taken seriously, revelation becomes more important, not less.

Reason may infer that God is one, eternal, necessary or omniscient. But there are truths about God—His purposes, His acts, His relation to us, and what Christians mean by speaking of Father and Son—which reason could never discover from creation alone.

If we are to know God more fully, God must make Himself known.

The movement of the argument has therefore reversed. We began with only God knows as an objection: God knows something Jesus does not. We then discovered something deeper: only God knows what it is to know as God knows.

That leads to a third meaning. If only God knows God from within, then only God can make God known. The necessity of mystery therefore becomes the necessity of revelation.

But revelation creates a new question.

How do we know that a claimed revelation is really revelation?

Reason cannot answer by deriving the content of revelation independently. If it could, revelation would add nothing. Nor is it enough for a scripture simply to declare itself divine or divinely preserved. Such claims may be true, but they cannot authenticate themselves without circularity.

A claim to revelation may be examined for coherence with what reason already knows of God and of the moral order.

We may ask whether the messenger and the message bear signs that give rational grounds for recognising a divine origin.

And because revelation enters human life through persons, words and events, its public claims may be examined historically.

History is therefore not the sole judge of revelation, but it becomes indispensable wherever revelation makes claims about what God has done, whom He has sent, what those messengers taught and did, and how their testimony has reached us.

Even the moral test eventually enters history: if a prophet is presented not merely as a teacher but as an example, reason must ask not only what he taught, but what kind of life he actually lived.

Reason cannot tell us everything that God may reveal, nor can it demand that revelation contain only what reason could have discovered for itself.

Its task is more modest: to ask whether there are rational grounds for believing that God has in fact spoken.

Reason reaches its limit before the divine essence, but it need not be passive before a claim to revelation.

One God

Revelation gives knowledge. But it gives that knowledge in words, and words do not interpret themselves. No single term contains a complete doctrine of God; it must be understood within the larger body of revelation in which it stands.

At the beginning of this essay, we set aside the proposed cultural and linguistic explanations of Mark. We allowed does not know to retain its ordinary force. We can now return to the question with something established: allowing a word to speak does not make it self-interpreting.

Judaism, Christianity and Islam all confess that God is one.

But what does one mean when said of God?

God is not one being among other beings, as one man stands beside another. His unity cannot simply be imagined as a larger version of creaturely singularity.

The Jewish Scriptures already insist upon the unity of God while giving the Son of Man divine prerogatives—a conjunction which helps explain how Christianity could arise and take root within a Jewish world.

Islam brings the linguistic question into sharper focus because it names its doctrine of God tawḥīd and frequently presents that doctrine as the plain meaning of true monotheism.

But tawḥīd is not the Arabic word for one. The Qurʾān calls God wāḥid and aḥad. It does not use the noun tawḥīd.

Tawḥīd is the verbal noun of waḥḥada. In ordinary use, the verb means to make one, to unite or to bring together. Countries may be unified. Separate parts may be made into one.

Applied to God, that natural sense is clearly impossible. Islamic theology does not teach that Allah was divided and then unified, or that separate parts were combined to produce Him.

The lexicons therefore record a specifically theological use: waḥḥada Allāha, to declare or profess that God is one. Tawḥīd accordingly comes to mean the affirmation of God’s absolute unity.

That is real Arabic usage. But it is not the ordinary meaning operating without interpretation. It is a specialized religious use in which theology tells us how the word must be understood.

There is nothing necessarily illegitimate about that development. Christianity also uses technical words not found in Scripture to express what it believes Scripture reveals.

But the distinction matters. Tawḥīd cannot be presented as the untouched meaning of an Arabic word while Trinity is dismissed as theological construction.

The same movement appears in Islam’s most familiar proclamation:

Allāhu akbar.

It is commonly rendered, “God is great.” But the Arabic word for great is kabīr. Akbar is its elative form: greater or greatest.

The grammar therefore raises a question.

Greater than what?

The phrase itself does not say. Islamic theology completes it: Allah is greater than everything, beyond every creature and greater than anything the human mind can conceive. “God is greatest” expresses that received meaning. “God is great” removes the comparison contained in the Arabic form.

Once again, doctrine governs how the words are understood. Allah is not being ranked alongside rival gods or compared with creation as though He belonged to the same order of being. Creaturely language is being made to express a divine reality which exceeds its ordinary frame.

This does not refute tawḥīd.

It shows that tawḥīd is theology.

Trinity is likewise not a rival dictionary definition of one. It is Christianity’s doctrinal account of how the one God revealed in Scripture is Father, Son and Spirit.

The two accounts need not be equally true. But neither can be established merely by repeating the word one. Each must be judged by its coherence with the revelation from which it claims to arise.

That returns us to Mark.

We need not change does not know into makes known. The words may retain their ordinary force. But their ordinary force does not, by itself, determine the identity of the person of whom they are spoken.

The verse tells us something real about the Son.

The larger revelation tells us who the Son is.

The disagreement must therefore move beyond the meaning of isolated words. Christianity and Islam do not merely interpret the same sentence differently. They receive different accounts of Jesus and make competing claims about what God has revealed concerning Him.

Reason can examine the coherence of those claims.

History can examine where they came from.

That brings us to Jesus.

Jesus

Judaism and Christianity have already presented us with a dispute over interpretation. They receive the Scriptures of Israel but disagree about what those Scriptures disclose concerning Jesus.

Between Christianity and Islam, the disagreement goes further. It concerns not only how revelation should be interpreted, but what God revealed and which texts preserve it.

Christianity confesses that Jesus was crucified, raised from the dead and revealed as the eternal Son of God made flesh. The Qurʾān gives Jesus an extraordinarily prominent place: He is the Messiah, miraculously born of Mary, a Word from Allah, a spirit from Him, a worker of miracles and one whom Allah raised to Himself. Yet it denies that He is divine or the Son of God in the Christian sense, and says that His enemies neither killed nor crucified Him.

These are theological claims. But they also make contact with history.

The crucifixion is a claim about an event. The identity of the Injīl is a claim about revelation and text. The place given to Jesus by the first Christians is a claim about what the earliest witnesses believed and proclaimed.

The revelation claimed, the event denied and the texts contested therefore all enter historical-critical inquiry.

History cannot determine by itself whether a text is inspired or whether Jesus is divine. But it can ask when competing accounts appear, how closely they stand to the events they describe, which earlier sources they preserve and whether an alleged corruption has left evidence in the textual record.

Neither Christianity nor Islam can establish its account merely by appealing to the divine authority of its own scripture, because that authority is itself the matter to be established. If the Qurʾān is revelation, it may correct human witnesses, but it cannot make false what God had already revealed. Nor can the correction serve as proof that the Qurʾān is revelation. The relationship between that correction, the Injīl and the surviving Christian texts must therefore be explained.

The historical record must therefore be allowed to speak.

What, then, does it show?

The Qurʾān is an early witness to Islam, but not a historically proximate witness to Jesus. It appears centuries after the earliest Christian sources and gives a markedly different account of His identity and fate.

A common response, especially in contemporary Muslim apologetics, is that the original Injīl given to Jesus was subsequently lost, corrupted or misunderstood.

But what is this Injīl? The Qurʾān tells the People of the Gospel to judge by what God revealed in it and speaks of what they find written in the Torah and the Gospel. Does the Injīl correspond to the Gospel preserved in the four canonical accounts—according to Matthew, Mark, Luke and John—or to an altogether different text?

If it is a different text, was it ever written down? Where can it be found, and what documentary trace has it left? If it is preserved within the canonical Gospels, what precisely was corrupted, when did the corruption occur and how did it spread across the surviving witnesses?

The method can be tested within Mark itself. If Mark 13:32 is cited as reliable evidence concerning Jesus, what historical principle permits that verse to be isolated from the same chapter’s presentation of the divine, messianic Son of Man?

The claim of corruption therefore does not close the historical inquiry.

It enlarges it.

What credible evidence, broadly accepted in historical scholarship, supports the existence of the alternative account? What comparably early source preserves the Qurʾānic denial of the crucifixion?

The same inquiry must be applied to Christianity. Its documentary record has been subjected to sustained historical scrutiny. Paul’s undisputed letters take us into the earliest decades of the movement and into contact with its first leaders. Critical scholarship ordinarily dates the canonical Gospels to the same century. Within that earliest documentary deposit, the crucifixion stands at the centre of the Christian proclamation, belief in the Resurrection is already established, and Jesus is confessed as Lord within the worshipping life of the first communities.

None of that by itself proves the full Christian claim. Historical method cannot establish that a text is inspired, that the Resurrection was an act of God or that Jesus is the eternal Son.

But it can establish which account is historically better grounded.

On the questions at issue here, the Christian record has undergone the test. The Islamic correction has yet to supply comparable historical evidence.

And now the question posed by Mark can be stated more precisely.

When read literally, the verse records a real limit in the Son’s knowledge. What it cannot determine by itself is who that Son is.

That question must be answered from the testimony that preserved the verse and from the historical record in which it stands. The inquiry is whether Christ’s divinity and humanity were invented to rescue Mark 13:32 or belong to the revelation within which Mark 13:32 must be read.

The verse remains.

But it cannot independently overturn the identity disclosed by the same revelation that gives us the verse.

The essence of God remains beyond our comprehension.

The footprint of revelation does not.

We began with three words:

Only God knows.

At first they seemed to close the case against Christ. Instead, they opened a much larger question.

Only God comprehends divine knowledge from within.

Only God knows God from within.

And therefore, if we are to know what reason alone cannot reach, only God can reveal God.

History cannot stand inside the divine essence. But revelation, if it has entered the world, leaves evidence behind.

The final question is whether God has spoken—and, if He has, where that evidence leads.

Only God knows.

But we can know what He has revealed.
